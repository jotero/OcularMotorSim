# From Circuit to Binocular Behavior: An Integrated Virtual Oculomotor System for Eye-Movement Disorders

## Specific Aims

Eye movements make otherwise hidden physiology visible. They reveal how the brain senses motion, selects and tracks targets, stabilizes gaze, and coordinates the two eyes. Yet an abnormal movement is rarely explained by its appearance alone. Similar patterns can arise from injury to the vestibular organs, cerebellum, brainstem pathways, motor nerves, or ocular motor apparatus, and compensation can obscure the original deficit. Clinicians distinguish these possibilities by comparing responses across tasks, but most computational models represent only one kind of eye movement at a time. Data-driven methods offer a complementary route, yet uncommon and heterogeneous disorders rarely provide large cohorts labeled by underlying mechanism. A mechanistic simulation cannot replace patient data, but it can supply physiological constraints and explicit, testable hypotheses where data are sparse. We therefore lack a unified way to ask **which mechanism produced an observed binocular pattern and which additional test would be most informative.**

We developed a **Virtual Oculomotor System** to address this problem. One closed-loop simulation transforms bilateral visual and vestibular signals through interacting premotor, cerebellar, nuclear, and nerve pathways into 3-D movements of both eyes. A saccade changes the retinal errors seen by pursuit and vergence; target distance changes translational vestibular demands; blur and disparity jointly shape the near response. The simulator preserves the latent mechanisms behind every trajectory and supports graded, lateralized lesions. Its benchmark framework measures 94 outputs against 95 prespecified physiological ranges. The record is informative rather than uniformly positive: 15 stored metrics are outside range, pathological tests remain largely qualitative, and no method yet determines which latent mechanisms are recoverable.

The **overall objective** of this exploratory R21 is to establish a rigorously bounded, integrated simulation of the oculomotor system and use it to determine when testing across behaviors can distinguish competing causes of abnormal eye movements. Our **central hypothesis** is that requiring many eye movements to share one biological framework will expose constraints invisible in isolated models, and that responses across systems will carry more information about cause than any single test. We will test this hypothesis in two aims.

### Aim 1. Test whether one integrated model can account for canonical and cross-system behavior under a common parameterization.

The standard will be deliberately demanding: **one model, one parameter set, many behaviors**. We will rebuild the benchmark record, resolve or delimit existing failures, and quantify demonstrations that are now qualitative. Evidence will distinguish implementation checks, literature-constrained benchmarks, and evaluation targets not used for calibration. Canonical responses will be joined by interactions that isolated models cannot confront: visual suppression of vestibular responses, saccade-vergence coordination, distance scaling of translational VOR, and accommodation-vergence dissociations. Prespecified metrics, parameter provenance, fixed seeds, and separation of calibration from evaluation will reduce circularity. Rather than tune every discrepancy away, sensitivity analyses will identify failures attributable to uncertain components, including the reduced ocular plant, and define where the model should not be trusted. **Expected outcome:** a reproducible map of what the architecture explains, where it fails, and which additional mechanisms are justified: a quantitative domain of validity rather than a claim of universal realism.

### Aim 2. Use cross-system responses to identify pathological mechanisms and select the next informative test.

A simulator with known latent causes provides a controlled setting for asking when mechanisms become observationally equivalent. Three exemplars become more informative when systems are tested together. In internuclear ophthalmoplegia, saccades versus convergence separate internuclear from shared motor deficits. In unilateral vestibular hypofunction, darkness, fixation, rotation, and head impulses constrain peripheral loss, velocity storage, and compensation. In vergence-accommodation disorders, independently manipulating disparity, blur, distance, prisms, and lenses separates pathways producing the same near deviation. We will infer targeted parameter subsets, add adaptation only where a defined error signal and time course can be tested, and quantify recovery and residual uncertainty. Priors for lesion class, laterality, and time since onset will be introduced incrementally, and tests ranked by uncertainty reduction. Recovery under parameter and model mismatch will guard against succeeding only because the inference model generated its own data. **Expected outcome:** identifiable parameter combinations, explicit equivalence classes that eye movements cannot resolve, and a minimal next-test strategy for each exemplar.

Successful completion will establish a scientific instrument for studying interactions, not a claim of comprehensive diagnosis or perfect biomechanics. It will deliver a common simulation framework, transparent evidence for its limits, synthetic ensembles with known mechanisms, and a principled approach to test selection. It will also make hidden physiology visible: users can perturb a named pathway and follow the consequences from internal state to binocular behavior. This openly inspectable, natural-language-accessible **didactic laboratory** will connect circuit hypotheses to eye movements while keeping the LLM separate from the scientific core. The platform will support a future R01 testing whether mechanistic synthetic data improve inference in independent patient cohorts.
