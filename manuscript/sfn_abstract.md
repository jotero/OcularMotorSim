## Abstract — full version (for reference / poster intro; ~2,900 characters)

The neural control of eye movements integrates vestibular, visual, and oculomotor signals across an anatomically distributed network spanning the brainstem, cerebellum, and cortex. Most existing models capture a single behavior (e.g., the VOR or saccades) in isolation, which limits their use for teaching system-level principles or for generating data to constrain parameter-fitting pipelines. We present a unified, end-to-end simulator of the primate oculomotor system that reproduces the full repertoire of eye movements within a single architecture and parameter set.

The model is implemented in JAX as a continuous-time state-space system, following the signal flow from head velocity through the semicircular-canal array, velocity storage, neural integrator, and a binocular oculomotor plant to final eye position, with parallel visual pathways for smooth pursuit, optokinetic responses, vergence, and accommodation. Saccades are generated by a Robinson local-feedback burst circuit with explicit excitatory/inhibitory burst neurons and omnipause gating, and a cerebellar module supplies forward-model and gaze-holding signals. A 14-state final common pathway with separable lesion parameters reproduces clinically realistic deficits (e.g., internuclear ophthalmoplegia, cranial-nerve palsies). With a single default parameter set the simulator reproduces VOR and velocity-storage time-constant extension, optokinetic nystagmus and afternystagmus, the saccadic main sequence and refractory period, smooth pursuit, fixational eye movements, ocular counter-roll, and vergence.

Because the model is fully differentiable, every parameter—time constants, gains, and pathway-specific lesion knobs—is biophysically interpretable and recoverable by gradient-based fitting. We highlight two applications. First, an interactive web tool translates plain-language scenarios (e.g., "patient with left vestibular neuritis during a head-impulse test") into simulations and annotated figures through a language-model interface, providing an accessible educational platform for students and clinicians to explore normal and pathological eye movements. Second, the simulator serves as a synthetic-data generator: by simulating responses under known ground-truth parameters and noise, it produces labeled datasets for validating parameter-recovery and disease-classification methods before application to patient recordings. The complete codebase, benchmark suite, and web tool are openly available.
